The Psychology of Money by Morgan Housel in two minutes.

Most people believe getting rich is about finance formulas, spreadsheets, and being a genius. In reality, money is not a science. It is a psychological mirror.

Ronald Read was an ordinary janitor who quietly saved modest wages into index funds. When he died, he left behind eight million dollars. Meanwhile, Richard Fuscone was a Harvard-educated Wall Street executive who borrowed heavily, built a mansion, and went completely bankrupt. Financial success is not about what you know. It is about how you behave.

First, beware the Man in the Car Paradox. When you see someone driving a Ferrari, you almost never think, wow, look at that driver. You think, if I had that car, everyone would admire me. Here is the paradox: nobody is looking at you. They are only imagining themselves. Rich is current income and spent money. Fast cars, luxury watches, and massive mortgages. Wealth is what you do not see. It is unspent cash, financial options, and the quiet freedom to say no.

Second, understand confounding compounding. Warren Buffett is famous as the world's greatest investor, but his real superpower is not intelligence. It is time. Over ninety-nine percent of his wealth was accumulated after his fifty-sixth birthday. Compounding works like a snowball rolling down an endless mountain. The math only explodes if you let the snowball roll for decades without stepping in front of it.

That leads to the ultimate rule: room for error. The most important part of any financial plan is planning for your plan not going according to plan. Survival is the only math that matters. If you risk what you need to chase what you do not need, one bad crash will wipe you out completely.

Finally, master the goalpost of enough. If your expectations rise with every dollar you earn, you will run on a hedonic treadmill forever. True wealth is not buying luxury toys to impress strangers. True wealth is the ability to wake up every morning and say: I can do whatever I want today, with whoever I want, for as long as I want.

So tell me below. Would you rather have ten million dollars in the bank, but work ninety hours a week for a brutal boss? Or have one hundred thousand dollars, but own every single second of your time?
